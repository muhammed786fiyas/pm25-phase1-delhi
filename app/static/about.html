About this data

What the numbers are, how they were checked, and where they should not be trusted.

What this is

A machine-learning model (LightGBM) that estimates daily mean PM2.5 anywhere in Delhi from satellite and meteorological inputs, trained against 42 CPCB ground monitors. The study window is 2025-03-01 to 2026-02-28.

Estimates are produced on a 1 km grid of 2,388 cells. Each cell's value comes from its own aerosol optical depth, meteorology, vegetation and land cover — it is not interpolated from nearby monitors.

This is retrospective, not live. It cannot report current air quality. Its inputs are ERA5 reanalysis (about five days behind) and MAIAC satellite AOD (one to three days). It exists to reconstruct the past for research, not to tell you whether to go outside today.

How it was validated

Performance is measured by spatial leave-one-station-out cross-validation: each station is held out in turn, along with any other station within 2 km, and predicted by a model that never saw it. That simulates the real task — estimating somewhere with no monitor.

The random-CV column is deliberately included as a warning: it is the number a naive validation would report, and it is inflated by about 0.08 R² because held-out days leak information about their own station. 0.8018 is the honest figure.

Is the grid itself trustworthy?

Grid cells have no ground truth, so the pipeline was checked a different way: does the cell containing a station predict what the station predicts? Across 13,595 station-days the correlation is 0.9901 with a median difference of −0.11 µg/m³. The near-zero bias is the meaningful part — a mis-joined feature or shifted date would show up as a systematic offset.

What the interval means

Every estimate carries a 90% prediction interval of roughly ±55%, produced by normalized conformal prediction: interval = prediction × (1 ± 0.5497). The factor is calibrated on out-of-fold errors at stations the model never trained on.

It is multiplicative because the error is: the relative error is roughly stable across the concentration range while the absolute error is not. A Gaussian ±1.645×RMSE interval was rejected — the residuals have kurtosis 17.6, so it would be far too wide mid-range and too narrow in the tail, which for air quality is the worse failure.

Coverage is marginal, not conditional. The interval is right about 90% of the time on average across all predictions — not at every location. Measured per station it ranges from 72.0% to 99.1%, with 18 of 42 stations below the 90% target. The under-covered ones are where column AOD tracks surface PM2.5 weakly, a property that cannot be computed for an unmonitored location. Widening the interval enough to protect the worst station would need ±105%, nearly doubling every estimate's range.

What is not used as a confidence measure

Distance to the nearest monitor is reported, but it is not a confidence grade. An earlier version of this app graded confidence by distance; that was tested against actual per-fold error and found unsupported (r = 0.149, p = 0.33, n = 42) and removed. Distance still limits scope — nothing is served beyond 15 km from a monitor — because the absence of a correlation across the 0.67–10.6 km the network spans does not license unlimited extrapolation.

Where the model is guessing

Every CPCB monitor is urban. Rural cells therefore sit outside anything the model was trained on — not by oversight, but by construction. 585 of 2,388 cells are materially outside the training range, and 93 are more than a full training-range width beyond it.

Those cells are drawn with red hatching on the map. Two things are wrong there, and both matter:

  • LightGBM cannot extrapolate. A tree fitted on cropland up to 78% has no split above 78%, so a 97%-cropland cell silently receives the boundary prediction.
  • The interval was never validated there. Its 90% coverage was measured at 42 urban stations; it is not evidence about farmland.

The intervals are deliberately not widened for these cells. Whether being unusual predicts worse coverage was tested against the stations' measured coverage: one of four checks passed, and that one was driven entirely by a single station sitting 102 range-widths out. Any widening factor would therefore have been invented, and an invented correction that looks rigorous is worse than an honest flag.

Coarser grids, and the trap in them

The 2, 5 and 10 km products are averages of 1 km cells. Their intervals are narrower, because averaging cancels some error:

ResolutionCells averagedInterval
1 km1±55.0%
2 km4±29.5%
5 km25±16.3%
10 km100±13.4%
A narrower interval does not mean better data for your street. It describes the average over the whole cell. One real 5 km cell reports 282.9 µg/m³ with an interval of 237–329, while the 25 one-kilometre cells inside it range from 201 to 327 — the lowest falls outside the coarse interval entirely. For a specific location, use 1 km and accept its wider range.

How much the interval may shrink is measured, not assumed. It depends on ρ, the same-day correlation between two locations' prediction errors, measured across all 861 station pairs: ρ = 0.050, bootstrap 95% CI [0.022, 0.085]. Both bounds ship in every coarse download so the uncertainty in the shrinkage is visible.

ρ does not decay with distance (p = 0.62), which means it is a whole-day effect rather than a local one — on 2025-05-22, 35 of 38 stations erred in the same direction by an average of 29%. Such an error cannot be averaged away, so the benefit of aggregating has a floor at √ρ ≈ 0.22. Beyond about 5 km, coarser cells buy almost no extra precision — they are worth using for smaller files, not better numbers.

Coarse products ship no features, deliberately. The model is nonlinear, so the model applied to averaged features does not equal the average of its predictions. Supplying both would invite a reproduction attempt that cannot succeed. For features, use the 1 km product — re-running the model on those reproduces the estimate exactly.

In the station and 1 km downloads, aod_gap_filled means "not a MAIAC retrieval" — not "a value was imputed". There are three cases:
